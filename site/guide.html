PROJECT NOTES / FIRST DRAFT

A portable identity.
A shared basis for trust.

Why Open2Artist exists, what it proposes, and what the first experiment must prove.

IN THIS DOCUMENTThe goalThe problemThe proposed solutionArtist control & paymentWhy these technologiesWhy nowScope & status

01 / PURPOSE

Let recognition travel with the participant.

O2A is a proposed open protocol for artists, venues, promoters, and events to carry signed evidence of who they represent and how they work together across applications.

An artist should be able to originate identity claims, authorize representatives, and present a portable history to a new counterparty. A compatible verifier should explain the claims, signatures, known conflicts, and policy behind its conclusion.

The goal: the same evidence, policy, protocol version, and evaluation context should produce the same result in independent implementations.

02 / THE PROBLEM

Many records. Different kinds of authority.

A world-class superstar may have different artist IDs on Spotify, Apple Music, and other services, plus separate metadata records in MusicBrainz, Wikidata, or ISNI. These references can be mapped, but they belong to different namespaces. The mapping itself does not prove the artist authorized it.

Artists can already claim platform profiles through provider approval. Community catalogs offer useful shared metadata. A standard ID helps disambiguate a public identity. O2A explores a further property: participant-signed authorization that can be verified outside the service that hosts the profile.

The same questions apply to venue operators, promoters, and organizers. A physical venue is distinct from its operator; a festival brand is distinct from an individual event; a staff account is distinct from an organization.

  • Ambiguity: identical names and changing roles can merge unrelated histories.
  • Fragmentation: evidence from one service may be difficult to inspect and reuse elsewhere.
  • Continuity: keys and representatives change, while historical authorization must remain checkable.
  • Disagreement: competing claims and corrections need an explicit, durable record.
  • Control: a profile association is different from a participant's signed consent.

O2A aims to complement useful identifiers and standards. Its value must come from authorization and reusable evidence, not merely adding another number.

03 / THE SOLUTION

Separate identity, evidence, and recognition.

  1. Identity: a stable EntityID and explicit controller authority.
  2. Evidence: signed claims, independent attestations, and challenges or revocations.
  3. Recognition: a named, versioned policy evaluates a specified evidence set and explains its result.

The first scenario involves a generic artist and venue signing evidence about the same canonical event manifest. A promoter may add booking evidence. Independent clients receive the same package and evaluate the same policy.

A signature establishes authorship and integrity. It does not establish that a performance occurred, that a participant owns a name, or that several signers are independent. Those are separate evidence and policy questions.

Policies must account for coordinated fake identities, conflicts, and missing evidence. A package cannot prove that no undiscovered challenge exists elsewhere. The design must keep that limitation visible instead of hiding it behind a universal score.

Registries are rebuildable projections over evidence. Destroying a search index should not destroy an independently retained history or alter verification of the same package.

04 / ARTIST VALUE

Signed approval with direct remuneration.

A proposed application lets an artist approve a particular use of profile, brand, catalog, or event material and receive payment at the authorization step. Examples include a commercial tour card, an approved promotional mention, or a maintained catalog feed.

A “metalink” here means a link accompanied by signed metadata describing the material, permitted use, terms, and authorization. The participant can choose the price, scope, duration, and payment destination. Offers can also be free.

  1. The artist or an authorized representative signs a specific offer.
  2. The publisher accepts the terms and pays an invoice tied to that offer.
  3. The publisher receives a signed permission grant associated with the payment receipt.
  4. A verifier checks that the use is within the grant's scope.

Sats over Lightning are the first payment candidate to test. A later, compatible swap adapter could allow receipt of a supported USDt asset. RGB contracts could represent selected digital rights where their state-transition rules are useful.

This is an opt-in relationship between participants. O2A cannot charge unrelated websites for public mentions or establish exclusive rights merely because someone controls an identity key.

Payment received, permission delivered, and optional asset conversion are distinct outcomes. A production implementation would need restart recovery, idempotent processing, and an explicit remedy when payment succeeds but delivery fails. Immediate completion, artist income, and adoption are hypotheses to test.

05 / TECHNOLOGY

Use each layer for what it can prove.

Signatures, canonical encoding, and policy

These provide independently checkable authorship, stable evidence references, and reproducible decisions. The proposed Rust core must still be tested against independent implementations. Language choice is not proof of protocol correctness.

Conventional services and optional Pubky

APIs and database projections make discovery practical. Pubky / PKARR is a candidate for public-profile storage and discovery; it must pass migration and recovery tests. O2A identity must remain stable independently of a Pubky key, and private proof material must stay out of public profile storage.

Lightning, RGB, and Bitcoin

Lightning is a candidate for small payments. RGB clients validate off-chain contract state linked to Bitcoin commitments and UTXO seals. These properties may support rights or asset transitions; basic identity claims do not require every assertion to become a transaction.

Client-side validation is not a swap mechanism by itself. Any USDt integration needs an authenticated asset, compatible wallets, liquidity, recipient checks, and tested completion and recovery rules. No production USDt route is assumed by this draft.

06 / WHY NOW

A current concern. A bounded experiment.

Artist identity and authorization are active industry concerns. Existing identifier standards, credential models, public-key discovery tools, and payment protocols provide components to evaluate without inventing every layer.

The opportunity is to test a small proposition: can one artist and one venue exchange useful evidence that two independent clients evaluate consistently? A later paid-use experiment can test whether direct control and remuneration make participation worthwhile.

Success must include actual usability: less repeated verification work, understandable disputes, workable key recovery, and willingness to provide evidence. A technically correct demonstration alone does not establish demand.

Further reading: DDEX party identifiers · W3C Verifiable Credentials · Pubky protocol · RGB client-side validation · Lightning paid-access patterns.

07 / STATUS

A first draft, open to testing.

As of September 2026, O2A is in design and specification. Schemas, canonical serialization, controller transitions, and conformance vectors precede a production implementation. This site describes the proposal and includes a teaching simulation, not a working verifier or payment service.

The initial focus is identity, evidence, and verification. Public-profile discovery, paid permissions, GatePass ticketing, SplitNight allocations, and settlement adapters are downstream work with separate acceptance gates.

All artist examples are generic. No real artist names, account IDs, endorsements, or customer claims are used. This static draft has no account creation, analytics, wallet connection, or payment collection.
